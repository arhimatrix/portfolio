Horizon Protocol is an ongoing research project developing the architectural masterplan and human-machine interface architecture for a permanent 100-person Moon Village, in collaboration with Prof. Kazuya Yoshida and the Space Robotics Laboratory at Tohoku University. The project integrates in-situ 3D-printed construction, sensor-embedded structural skins, and decentralised supervisory control into a single cyber-physical habitat system — treating the settlement itself as a robotic construction site that humans must remain legible to.
  • Designing SAMI — a wearable-sensor-informed kinetic architectural skin that reads occupant biometric and movement data in real time to adapt spatial configuration, luminance, and atmospheric zones.
  • Engineering 'Reflex Arc' distributed control loops: rule-based autonomous agents regulating life-support, pressure zones, and spatial reconfiguration in response to physiological sensor input — with human supervisory override.
  • Developing the in-situ robotic construction strategy: 3D-printed ISRU regolith shells with embedded piezoelectric sensing, and autonomous robotic responders applying targeted material reinforcement.
  • Developing human-in-the-loop interface dashboards communicating robot intent, safety states, and task sequences — enabling crew to monitor autonomous operations and issue override commands.
  • Testing campaign at the Tohoku University Space Robotics Laboratory (Prof. Kazuya Yoshida): the piezoelectric sensing matrix, SAMI skin, and Reflex Arc control logic will be validated, alongside cooperative robotic assembly of inflatable and deployable structures.
  • Human performance and interface evaluation protocols will be evaluated through structured user studies during the AATC Poland analog campaign in August 2026, where I serve as Human Performance Lead.

01. Deep Engineering: Intelligent Structural Sensing
Piezoelectric Regolith Integration

Terrestrial architecture relies on static mass to resist environmental loads; extreme extraterrestrial environments demand that infrastructure functions as an active, self-organizing system of autonomous agents. By embedding a dense, continuous matrix of piezoelectric sensors directly within the 3D-printed ISRU (In-Situ Resource Utilization) lunar concrete shell, the passive shield is transformed into a reactive, cyber-physical membrane. This multi-layered enclosure operates as a localized nervous system, continuously translating mechanical stresses, vibrations, and structural deformations into real-time telemetry.

The "Acoustic" Building: The Reflex Arc

Instead of merely reacting to structural failure after it occurs, the system utilizes a biological "reflex arc" framework to achieve predictive habitability. Acoustic emission sensors and piezoelectric nodes capture the high-frequency structural signatures generated by extreme 300°C thermal cycling and micrometeoroid impacts. This continuous sensory stream allows the underlying system model to predict structural fatigue, localize impact coordinates, and calculate micro-fissure propagation vectors before localized failures can breach the pressurized habitat envelope.

Structural Adaptation & Preemptive Mitigation

The architectural geometry dynamically responds to real-time predictive hazard overlays through a dual-layered mitigation strategy:

  • Sub-Structure Optimization: Adaptive foundations utilize active kinetic components to shift load distributions and modify grounding profiles, directly mitigating the intense structural shear caused by extreme lunar thermal expansion and contraction.
  • Enclosure Thickening & Material Deposition: Triggered by high-risk predictive modeling, autonomous robotic responders can apply targeted material reinforcement to high-stress zones. The shell dynamically thickens its outer protective layers in direct response to directional micrometeoroid flux and localized material degradation.

Together, these strategies frame the habitat shell as autonomous additive construction with in-situ materials — a structure that robotic agents print, monitor, and reinforce continuously. Cooperative robotic assembly of inflatable and deployable structures will be tested in collaboration with the Tohoku University Space Robotics Laboratory.

The Central Problem: Behavioral Legibility in Autonomous Hubs

A fully autonomous hub removes the human operator layer entirely — occupants must infer system state and vehicle behavior from spatial and architectural cues alone. The design methodology began with behavioral mapping: occupant trace studies and user studies evaluating different interface configurations, informing a multi-layered digital and spatial system:

Integration of Digital Operations with Physical Space
  • Real-Time Occupant Monitoring: Sensor networks track pedestrian density and flow velocity, feeding predictive occupancy models for real-time routing and environmental control.
  • Adaptive Interface Logic: Wayfinding adjusts spatial indicators based on live occupant behavior and vehicle scheduling — replacing static signage with context-aware navigation cues.
  • Synchronized Safety Zones: Vehicle approach states and docking sequences communicated through coordinated spatial, acoustic, and visual signals.
From Strategy to Computational Form: Scaling Legible Interfaces
  • Occupancy-Responsive Geometry: Structural ribs and clearance profiles parametrically linked to pedestrian density — high-traffic zones received extended canopy coverage and enhanced visual contrast.
  • Manufacturing Scalability: Parametric definitions generated site-specific configurations maintaining a consistent structural typology, exporting fabrication-ready geometry for additive manufacturing and CNC.
  • Interface Coherence: Color gradients, rib spacing, and material transitions synchronized across all hub sites, creating a unified experiential language adapting to local site constraints.

The Tel Aviv Red Line is a hybrid light-rail system integrating manual operation on the main line with full automation in the underground tunnel section. The automated tunnel segment — 23 kilometres where thousands of passengers interact daily with driverless vehicles — presents a distinct human-machine collaboration challenge: designing spatial and interface systems that function seamlessly within a larger manual-operation context, and enabling passengers to understand and trust autonomous operation when the same line elsewhere is driver-operated.

  • Conducted user research and behavioral observation to understand how passengers perceive the transition from manual to automated vehicle operation, and how they interpret safety boundaries and vehicle intent in fully driverless tunnel stations.
  • Designed spatial architecture and interface systems communicating both vehicle state and operational mode: platform screen doors, wayfinding, acoustic cues, and real-time information displays adapted for automated tunnel operation.
  • Maintained strict compliance with safety guidelines, fire egress regulations, and accessibility standards while integrating automated transit hardware into underground station envelopes interfacing with manual-operation surface infrastructure.
  • Specified supervisory infrastructure and digital twin architectures enabling operators to monitor and coordinate both manual and automated vehicle operations in real time, with human-in-the-loop intervention capability.

Project Overview · Systems Architecture Lead
Robotic in-orbit assembly of complex spacecraft at the Lunar Gateway presents a fundamental human-machine collaboration challenge: modules manufactured by distributed contractors must be assembled by coordinated robot teams under human supervisory control — with no margin for error in microgravity.

The design strategy centred on standardized modular interfaces: defining exact mechanical, electrical, and informational specifications enabling distributed fabrication with standardized specifications across independent manufacturing chains. The work integrated interface design (docking geometry, electrical handshakes, capture mechanisms), computational algorithms for robotic assembly coordination (sequencing, tolerance budgeting, multi-robot consensus), and supervisory protocols enabling operators to monitor progress, recognise deviations, and intervene at contingency points.
  • Designed standardized modular interfaces specifying docking geometry, electrical connections, and informational handshakes — enabling distributed fabrication across independent contractors without custom fit-up, and making assembly state legible to human supervisory operators.
  • Applied evolutionary solvers optimising interface tolerances across distributed manufacturing, simulating worst-case cumulative stack-up to ensure capture mechanisms succeed and structural margins are preserved under all tolerance scenarios.
  • Programmed consensus-coordination algorithms for multi-robot assembly sequencing — leader election, human override protocols, anomaly recovery — validated through digital twin simulation of microgravity dynamics and docking loads.
  • Built real-time data pipelines exporting live fabrication tolerances and assembly states to digital twin dashboards, supporting user evaluation of supervisory interface legibility and contingency decision timelines.

System Architecture · Human-Robot Collaborative Mapping
Human-robot collaborative mapping on Mars-analogue terrain introduces a core supervisory control problem: a six-wheeled rover and an autonomous aerial drone must coordinate semi-autonomously across complex terrain while an Earth-based operator maintains situational awareness of both systems under communication lag. The operator must track robot position, coordination state, and sensor health — and retain the ability to issue override commands at any point in the mission.

The UMBRELLA system architecture addresses this challenge through two formally defined subsystems. The Mobility Subsystem governs localisation, path planning, and actuator control — providing the real-time telemetry stream the supervisory interface uses to display robot state. The Communications System manages inter-vehicle data-link, science data relay, and ground-segment uplink — ensuring the operator retains command authority and can monitor link health at all times. Both subsystems were formally defined in the MBSE framework so they could be validated in digital twin simulation before deployment.

Mantis · Computational Resilience Design · Designer-in-the-Loop
While earlier projects address autonomous robotic systems with human supervisory control, computational design represents a different HRC paradigm: algorithms augmenting architect expertise in evidence-based decision-making.

Buildings face compounding climate risks — wildfire, flood, hurricane, seismic loading, extreme heat — that traditional design methods cannot address simultaneously. Computational design explores hundreds of configurations simultaneously, identifying unintuitive resilient forms that human intuition alone would not reach.
  • —Developed Mantis to resolve the multi-hazard optimisation problem: integrating real-time geospatial risk feeds for any latitude/longitude input, computing a localized vulnerability index — replacing assumption-based design with evidence-based hazard profiling.
  • —Implemented parametric optimization testing 500+ building configurations per hazard profile, generating hazard-specific modules: low-profile hip roofs, reinforced seismic cores, intumescent timber treatments — targeting SDG 11 compliance.
  • —Maintained designer-in-the-loop control: Mantis surfaces optimal configurations for architect review — computation identifies candidates, the architect contextualises and approves.
