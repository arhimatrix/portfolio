Horizon Protocol is an ongoing research project developing the architectural masterplan and human-machine interface architecture for a permanent 100-person Moon Village, in collaboration with Prof. Kazuya Yoshida and the Space Robotics Laboratory at Tohoku University. The project integrates in-situ 3D-printed construction, sensor-embedded structural skins, and decentralised supervisory control into a single cyber-physical habitat system — treating the settlement itself as a robotic construction site that humans must remain legible to.
  • Designing SAMI — a wearable-sensor-informed kinetic architectural skin that reads occupant biometric and movement data in real time to adapt spatial configuration, luminance, and atmospheric zones.
  • Engineering 'Reflex Arc' distributed control loops: rule-based autonomous agents regulating life-support, pressure zones, and spatial reconfiguration in response to physiological sensor input — with human supervisory override.
  • Developing the in-situ robotic construction strategy: 3D-printed ISRU regolith shells with embedded piezoelectric sensing, and autonomous robotic responders applying targeted material reinforcement.
  • Developing human-in-the-loop interface dashboards communicating robot intent, safety states, and task sequences — enabling crew to monitor autonomous operations and issue override commands.
  • Testing campaign at the Tohoku University Space Robotics Laboratory (Prof. Kazuya Yoshida): the piezoelectric sensing matrix, SAMI skin, and Reflex Arc control logic will be validated, alongside cooperative robotic assembly of inflatable and deployable structures.
  • Human performance and interface evaluation protocols will be evaluated through structured user studies during the AATC Poland analog campaign in August 2026, where I serve as Human Performance Lead.
  • Findings to be presented at the Europlanet Science Congress (EPSC 2026) in The Hague, The Netherlands, September 2026.

01. Deep Engineering: Intelligent Structural Sensing
Piezoelectric Regolith Integration

Terrestrial architecture relies on static mass to resist environmental loads; extreme extraterrestrial environments demand that infrastructure functions as an active, self-organizing system of autonomous agents. By embedding a dense, continuous matrix of piezoelectric sensors directly within the 3D-printed ISRU (In-Situ Resource Utilization) lunar concrete shell, the passive shield is transformed into a reactive, cyber-physical membrane. This multi-layered enclosure operates as a localized nervous system, continuously translating mechanical stresses, vibrations, and structural deformations into real-time telemetry.

The "Acoustic" Building: The Reflex Arc

Instead of merely reacting to structural failure after it occurs, the system utilizes a biological "reflex arc" framework to achieve predictive habitability. Acoustic emission sensors and piezoelectric nodes capture the high-frequency structural signatures generated by extreme 300°C thermal cycling and micrometeoroid impacts. This continuous sensory stream allows the underlying system model to predict structural fatigue, localize impact coordinates, and calculate micro-fissure propagation vectors before localized failures can breach the pressurized habitat envelope.

Structural Adaptation & Preemptive Mitigation

The architectural geometry dynamically responds to real-time predictive hazard overlays through a dual-layered mitigation strategy:

  • Sub-Structure Optimization: Adaptive foundations utilize active kinetic components to shift load distributions and modify grounding profiles, directly mitigating the intense structural shear caused by extreme lunar thermal expansion and contraction.
  • Enclosure Thickening & Material Deposition: Triggered by high-risk predictive modeling, autonomous robotic responders can apply targeted material reinforcement to high-stress zones. The shell dynamically thickens its outer protective layers in direct response to directional micrometeoroid flux and localized material degradation.

Together, these strategies frame the habitat shell as autonomous additive construction with in-situ materials — a structure that robotic agents print, monitor, and reinforce continuously. Cooperative robotic assembly of inflatable and deployable structures will be tested in collaboration with the Tohoku University Space Robotics Laboratory.

NEOM’s 170km mobility network presents a core human-machine collaboration challenge: thousands of daily occupants must navigate driverless interchange hubs where autonomous vehicles, robotic maintenance systems, and human pedestrians share dynamic space. The Central Problem: Behavioral Legibility in Autonomous Hubs

In conventional transit hubs, human operators — visible staff, platform announcements, manual gate control — establish occupant confidence. A fully autonomous hub removes this layer entirely. Occupants must infer system state and vehicle behavior from spatial and architectural cues alone. The design methodology therefore began with behavioral mapping: ethnographic observation, occupant trace studies, and user studies evaluating different interface configurations, informing a multi-layered digital and spatial system:

Integration of Digital Operations with Physical Space

The architecture unifies two traditionally separate design layers: operational logistics (Back-of-House) and occupant experience (Front-of-House).

  • Real-Time Occupant Monitoring: Sensor networks track pedestrian density, flow velocity, and dwell patterns, feeding predictive occupancy models for real-time routing and environmental control.
  • Adaptive Interface Logic: Wayfinding dynamically adjusts spatial indicators based on live occupant behavior and vehicle scheduling — replacing static signage with context-aware navigation cues.
  • Synchronized Safety Zones: Vehicle approach states and docking sequences are communicated through coordinated spatial, acoustic, and visual signals, enabling occupants to anticipate autonomous movement.
From Strategy to Computational Form: Scaling Legible Interfaces

Scaling a coherent, legible interface across multiple hub sites with varying geometries and occupancy profiles required parametric exploration driven by three algorithmic objectives:

  • Occupancy-Responsive Geometry: Structural ribs, material thickness, and clearance profiles parametrically linked to pedestrian density and vehicle approach angles — high-traffic zones received extended canopy coverage and enhanced visual contrast.
  • Manufacturing Scalability: Parametric definitions generated site-specific configurations while maintaining a consistent structural typology, exporting fabrication-ready geometry for additive manufacturing and CNC assembly.
  • Interface Coherence: Color gradients, rib spacing, and material transitions were synchronized across all hub sites, creating a unified experiential language that adapted to local site constraints.

Project Overview · Transit Architecture Lead, WSP · 2016–2022
The Tel Aviv Red Line is a hybrid light-rail system integrating manual operation on the main line with full automation in the underground tunnel section. The automated tunnel segment — 23 kilometres where thousands of passengers interact daily with driverless vehicles — presents a distinct human-machine collaboration challenge: designing spatial and interface systems that function seamlessly within a larger manual-operation context, and enabling passengers to understand and trust autonomous operation when the same line elsewhere is driver-operated.

As Transit Architecture Lead at WSP, I designed the spatial and interface conditions of the human-machine encounter across three central underground stations, requiring three phases: (1) human factors research and interface legibility studies comparing manual and automated sections, (2) architectural design of spatial HRC systems for the tunnel, and (3) supervisory infrastructure and digital twin implementation enabling real-time coordination between manual and automated operations.
  • Conducted user research and behavioral observation to understand how passengers perceive the transition from manual to automated vehicle operation, and how they interpret safety boundaries and vehicle intent in fully driverless tunnel stations.
  • Designed spatial architecture and interface systems communicating both vehicle state and operational mode: platform screen doors, wayfinding, acoustic cues, and real-time information displays adapted for automated tunnel operation.
  • Maintained strict compliance with complex safety guidelines, fire egress regulations, and accessibility standards while integrating automated transit hardware into underground station envelopes that interface with manual-operation surface infrastructure.
  • Specified supervisory infrastructure and digital twin architectures enabling operators to monitor and coordinate both manual and automated vehicle operations in real time, with human-in-the-loop intervention capability.

Interface Challenge · Spatial HRC Design · Supervisory Systems
The Interface Challenge: Passenger Understanding of Hybrid Operation
A key complexity was the hybrid nature of the system. Passengers experience the same line in two operational modes: manual on surface sections and full automation in the tunnel. User research methods included operational mode perception studies, comparative behavioral observation across manual and automated stations, safety perception studies for PSD function, wayfinding legibility testing, and emergency response evaluation. This empirical grounding informed the full spatial and interface design system.

Spatial Architecture & HRC Design for Automated Tunnel
The underground stations were engineered as integrated HRC interface systems. Operational mode signaling used consistent architectural markers, material treatments, and lighting patterns to communicate automation state. Platform screen doors were validated through user studies to ensure reliable passenger interpretation of vehicle presence and boarding readiness. Wayfinding embedded spatial architecture itself as a navigation system — structural elements, material transitions, and floor patterns — reducing cognitive load without staff guidance. Accessibility compliance required redundant visual, acoustic, and tactile communication to ensure independence in automated stations.
  • Specified human-in-the-loop control room layouts, CCTV camera matrices, and hardware interfaces adapted for hybrid manual/automated operations, enabling coordinated supervision across both operational modes.
  • Developed structured asset databases mapping physical station components and vehicle systems to dynamic digital operation registries, translating BIM into active facility management.
  • Implemented real-time state monitoring, mode transition tracking, predictive maintenance, and incident response capabilities across both manual and automated sections of the line.
  • Conducted onsite commissioning and 1:1 scale validation of platform door tolerances, automated passenger communication systems, emergency protocols, and mode transition procedures under real passenger loads.
